Machine Learning for Power Transformer Dissolved Gas Analysis: A Critical Review of Validation Practices
Abstract
Dissolved gas analysis supports power transformer diagnosis, but machine learning results require careful interpretation when labels, datasets, and evaluation procedures differ. This critical review examines 44 eligible reports from a supplied document corpus, comprising 35 empirical studies and nine reviews. It organizes diagnostic pipelines by interpretation basis, task, learner family, data handling, and validation independence, and compares representative results within their original experimental settings. The synthesis identifies reused datasets, rule-generated labels, minority-fault weaknesses, test-assisted selection, and unresolved reporting discrepancies that limit comparisons between published scores. External benchmark use ranges from independent assessment to tuning or incremental adaptation, while temporal demonstrations address narrower forecasting questions than fleet-level failure prognosis. Statements about public data, code, explanations, or compact inference do not independently establish reproducibility or deployment readiness. The resulting research priorities include traceable benchmarks, grouped evaluation, training-confined augmentation, declared transfer policies, prospective forecasting, and outcome-linked decision support. Findings apply to the supplied corpus; original search provenance remains unavailable, remote releases were not authenticated, and reported experiments were not independently reproduced.
Index Terms— Condition monitoring, Dissolved gas analysis, Fault diagnosis, Machine learning, Power transformers.
I. Introduction
Failures in power transformers insulated with oil can interrupt supply, damage equipment and expose personnel and the environment to fire or oil-release hazards [1]. Because these assets are costly to replace and their insulation degrades under electrical stress, thermal loading and chemical aging, maintenance decisions require evidence of developing defects [1]. Periodic testing and continuous monitoring offer complementary information, but their usefulness depends on whether measurements support a reliable diagnosis [2]. This makes diagnostic validity an engineering requirement for maintenance planning.
Dissolved gas analysis (DGA) provides indirect evidence of insulation deterioration by examining gases associated with oil and cellulose decomposition [2]. Conventional interpretation uses key-gas patterns, gas ratios and geometric fault regions, including Rogers and Doernenburg ratios and Duval diagrams [1], [2]. These methods provide interpretable diagnostic criteria, yet ratio combinations may fall outside assigned codes, while measurements near region boundaries can produce uncertain classifications [1]. Different interpretations of the same sample can also disagree, and gas evidence generally does not identify the physical fault location [1]. Consequently, a categorical DGA output requires contextual interpretation and, where necessary, complementary tests.
Machine learning (ML) extends DGA interpretation through learned relationships between measured gases and diagnostic labels. The supplied studies investigated Gaussian-process classification [3], graph convolutional diagnosis [4] and recurrent gas forecasting followed by classification [5], illustrating different uses of gas composition and temporal information. However, model comparisons depend on label definitions, feature transformations and evaluation design. In the comparison of principal component analysis, abbreviated PCA, with autoencoder compression, latent dimensions were selected using test-set behavior [6]; an imbalance-handling study likewise used testing records to select oversampling settings [7]. Such practices mix model development with final assessment. Another study explicitly grouped repeated gas vectors during development and withheld external data from learner tuning [8], although its external challenge and measurement units still constrain interpretation. Reported performance therefore needs to be assessed alongside data independence and selection procedures before conclusions about diagnostic transfer can be drawn.
The supplied reviews cover several complementary perspectives. Monitoring-equipment research examined sensing technologies and installation requirements [2], while a high-voltage deep-learning review discussed transformer applications within a broader equipment scope [9]. A DGA-focused review brought together conventional interpretation, monitoring devices and learning methods [10]. A surrogate-model review examined physical, data-driven and hybrid approaches and explicitly discussed sample dependence, uncertainty and field validation [11]. These concerns already appear in prior reviews. Building on them, the present synthesis links individual study results to their source locations, dataset definitions, preprocessing boundaries and evaluation roles, making the evidence behind comparisons inspectable.
The review examines 44 eligible DGA reports from the supplied document inventory: 35 empirical studies and nine review-only papers. Related publications remain linked when they reuse datasets, and supplementary thesis material is not counted as an independent replication. The scope covers detection, diagnosis, condition assessment and gas-based forecasting; it does not imply exhaustive database-search coverage. Within this scope, the review provides four contributions:
1. A taxonomy connecting interpretation basis, diagnostic task, model design, data handling and validation.
2. A comparative analysis retaining dataset, label, metric and evaluation context for each result.
3. An evidence audit identifying dataset reuse, reporting conflicts and threats to independent assessment.
4. A research roadmap linking these gaps to reproducible evaluation and operational validation.
Section II presents DGA background, and Section III describes the review protocol. Sections IV–VI develop the taxonomy, comparative analysis and critical discussion. Section VII proposes research directions, and Section VIII concludes the review.
II. Background: DGA Fundamentals and Conventional Methods
DGA examines gases dissolved in insulating liquid to infer deterioration within a transformer. Electrical activity and heating can decompose oil or solid insulation, producing mixtures whose composition helps distinguish candidate fault mechanisms. Hydrogen (H₂) is associated with partial discharge, although it also occurs during arcing. Methane (CH₄), together with ethane (C₂H₆), contributes to signatures associated with milder heating; ethylene (C₂H₄) becomes more prominent under stronger thermal stress. Acetylene (C₂H₂) is an important indicator of energetic electrical discharge. The carbon oxides—monoxide (CO) and dioxide (CO₂)—provide evidence relevant to cellulose degradation. These associations describe patterns rather than exclusive chemical identifiers of individual faults [1], [2].
The common diagnostic vocabulary separates PD, denoting partial discharge, from discharge categories D1 and D2, associated respectively with lower and higher energy. Thermal categories distinguish T1, below 300 °C; T2, spanning 300–700 °C; and T3, above 700 °C. The supplied review reports these bands in its description of the Duval triangle [1]. Their precise boundary conventions require confirmation against the applicable standard [STANDARD - VERIFY CLAUSE/YEAR]. Cellulose overheating also warrants explicit attention: carbon-oxide evidence concerns solid insulation and should not be treated as interchangeable with hydrocarbon evidence about oil [2].
Conventional interpretation combines gas concentrations, ratios, and graphical regions. IEEE C57.104 and IEC 60599 are identified as interpretation frameworks in the supplied literature [6]; their applicable editions and clauses remain unverified [STANDARD - VERIFY CLAUSE/YEAR]. Key-gas diagnosis compares the prominent constituents with expected patterns for discharge or overheating [2]. Rogers and IEC schemes share three inputs: CH₄/H₂ compares methane with hydrogen; C₂H₂/C₂H₄ contrasts acetylene with ethylene; C₂H₄/C₂H₆ relates ethylene to ethane. However, their decision rules and reported class sets differ. Doernenburg retains the first two ratios and additionally considers C₂H₂/CH₄ plus C₂H₆/C₂H₂, giving four ratios [2]. Consequently, agreement cannot be assumed merely because methods share some gas inputs.
Duval Triangle 1 expresses CH₄, C₂H₄, and C₂H₂ as percentages of their combined concentration and places the resulting composition within diagnostic regions. Regions cover the discharge and thermal categories above, together with DT, which represents combined electrical and thermal activity [1], [2]. The original triangle assigns a diagnostic region without establishing an independent healthy category; its output therefore requires evidence that a fault interpretation is appropriate [1]. The pentagon incorporates H₂ and C₂H₆ alongside the triangle’s constituents, extending the graphical representation to five gases [2]. Table I contrasts these representations with learned models without implying an accuracy ranking.
Several limitations motivate ML. Ratio rules can leave some observations without a matching diagnosis, while nearby graphical boundaries make decisions sensitive to composition [1]. Moreover, gas concentrations reflect oil volume, sampling intervals, transport, fault energy, and operating history; a measured pattern does not uniquely determine its cause [11]. ML offers a way to model interactions among gas features rather than prescribe every boundary manually. However, overlapping signatures, correlated inputs, and uncertain measurements remain relevant after a classifier is trained [6]. Learning a decision surface therefore does not remove the need to examine diagnostic labels and the conditions under which measurements were obtained.

III. Review Methodology
This study used a structured, corpus-based critical review of learning methods applied to DGA measurements from power transformers with oil insulation. Four research questions guided the synthesis: RQ1, which model families and interpretation strategies occur in the supplied corpus, and how do they compare under compatible evaluation conditions? RQ2, how do dataset provenance, input representations and class balancing affect the interpretation of reported performance? RQ3, how reproducible and transferable are the results across transformers, utilities and operating periods? RQ4, which evidence gaps should guide future diagnostic and prognostic research? These questions prioritize evaluation quality alongside model design.
The review used only the individually uploaded replacement documents recorded in the fixed P01–P80 inventory. The author’s original databases, search strings, search dates and intended publication-period limits were not supplied and remain [AUTHOR TO SUPPLY]. Accordingly, the work does not claim an independently reproducible database search or exhaustive field coverage. Search identification and screening totals remain [N] placeholders in the proposed PRISMA-inspired description.
Eligibility required transformer-specific DGA evidence addressing fault detection, classification, severity or health assessment, or gas-based forecasting, using learning, hybrid or probabilistic approaches. Relevant reviews were retained for contextual synthesis. Studies restricted to unrelated equipment, non-DGA measurements or dry-type transformer diagnosis were excluded from the core synthesis. Complementary thermography and relevant thesis passages were recorded separately. Missing full texts, abstract-only records and exact duplicates did not contribute independent quantitative evidence.
File verification accounted for 80 records: 20 unusable HTML error files and 60 PDFs, including two duplicate aliases. Of 58 distinct PDFs, 44 were eligible DGA candidates: 35 empirical studies and nine reviews. Their main narratives were reviewed through conclusion. Eligible reports were not assumed to represent independent datasets. Extraction recorded features, dataset size and labels, balancing, validation, metrics, source locations, limitations and access claims. Appraisal examined label independence, correlated samples, preprocessing isolation, test-based tuning and external validation. Dataset reuse and conflicting values were flagged; quantitative comparisons retained their original evaluation context rather than forming a cross-study accuracy ranking.
IV. Taxonomy and ML Approaches for DGA Diagnosis
A. Classical Classifiers and Tree Ensembles
Classical methods treat diagnosis as a mapping from gas measurements or engineered descriptors to labels. Logistic regression (LR), support-vector machines (SVM), nearest-neighbor classifiers, and decision trees provide different ways to separate overlapping patterns without requiring a deep representation. The comparative study by Santamaria-Bonfil and colleagues includes these families alongside automated pipelines on a literature-assembled corpus described as 821 cases across ten categories. Its class-proportion-preserving holdout and development cross-validation support comparison within that experiment, although the listed class frequencies do not reconcile with the stated corpus size [12]. Model choice must therefore be interpreted alongside data accounting and class coverage.
Tree ensembles combine decision rules and can accommodate interactions among gas features. In the Rio Tinto study, random forests (RF) and boosting methods operate on scheme-specific ratio representations and labels, using 1,702 records with a holdout and development cross-validation. Strong agreement in this setting chiefly demonstrates reproduction of the labeling schemes; it does not establish agreement with independently inspected faults [13]. Conversely, HIRA-DGA, a framework for hierarchical invariant representations with abstention, examines a logistic hierarchy with duplicate-grouped development evaluation and a separate source used without target tuning. Its external single-fault assessment omits normal cases, while mixed cases are handled separately; magnitude comparisons are also complicated by undocumented source units [8]. These examples favor evaluating the complete labeling and validation pipeline over assigning a preferred classifier family.
B. Feedforward Networks and Representation Compression
Artificial neural networks, referred to here as ANN models, can learn nonlinear combinations of gas inputs, while preprocessing determines which information reaches the classifier. PCA provides linear compression; an autoencoder (AE) learns a reconstruction-based latent representation. In the controlled PCA–ANN versus AE–ANN comparison, both pipelines use 595 records, six fault categories, and a stratified partition containing 476 training and 119 test records. The supplied paper reports PCA–ANN accuracy of 68.9% [6, Table 4, p. 19], compared with 66.4% [6, Table 4, p. 19] from AE–ANN, but chooses latent dimensions using test performance [6]. This is an informative within-corpus comparison, with evaluation serving a development role; it cannot establish general superiority of linear compression.
Optimization can also modify an otherwise conventional network. The modified-bat backpropagation neural network (BPNN) study evaluates a small field corpus with a stated holdout, but its class-average table and prose give conflicting performance summaries [14]. The optimization layer therefore needs to be separated from the learner and from the aggregation of class-level results. For feedforward models, the central question is whether additional flexibility improves an independently assessed diagnostic target, rather than whether a more elaborate pipeline attains a larger development score.
C. Deep, Graph, and Sequence Models
Deep architectures pursue different representations rather than a single diagnostic strategy. The DNN, a deep neural network, trains on Duval-labeled utility measurements and evaluates an IEC benchmark after merging thermal categories. Its comparator confusion matrices use inconsistent totals, limiting direct score comparisons [15]. This illustrates why a large rule-labeled training collection does not resolve benchmark harmonization or establish inspected-fault truth.
The graph convolutional network (GCN) study constructs relationships among gas-feature vectors using learned similarity and neighborhood links. Its original corpus contains 718 cases spanning seven classes, partitioned into 538 training and 180 test records. GAN denotes a generative adversarial network; its expansion is described as confined to training, and the expanded-data experiment reports improved behavior; however, architecture and neighborhood choices are selected using test results [4]. The evidence thus combines a representation experiment with test-assisted development. Graph construction adds another fitting boundary that must be documented alongside scaling and model training.
The distilled convolutional neural network (CNN) study converts engineered gas descriptors into entropy-based and recurrence representations, then transfers a teacher’s behavior to smaller student networks. Its confidential field corpus and holdout support a study-local compression comparison, while preprocessing isolation and an untouched selection partition remain unclear [16]. Claims about compact inference should also be separated from demonstrated equipment deployment.
Sequence architectures require particular care about what constitutes a sequence. Wang and colleagues forecast chronological gas trajectories using LSTM, denoting long short-term memory, before downstream classification. Their reported deterioration at later forecast weeks concerns a future segment from one transformer, rather than cross-utility diagnosis [5]. By contrast, the study combining CNN, bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), and attention combines gas inputs with GAN augmentation and evaluates supplemented diagnostic examples [17]. Naming a recurrent component does not by itself establish prospective forecasting. Deep representation learning is useful to investigate, but its diagnostic meaning depends on sample construction, labels, and the forecast or classification target.
D. Fuzzy and Neuro-Fuzzy Interpretation
Fuzzy systems express gradual membership in gas-pattern categories rather than exclusively crisp boundaries. The Mamdani/key-gas framework combines gas percentages with an auxiliary cellulose criterion and evaluates literature-referenced cases without a separately documented development/test division. Conflicting descriptions of the auxiliary carbon-oxide criterion prevent adopting that rule uncritically [18]. Interpretability of a rule base therefore requires consistency between its stated chemistry and implemented decisions.
ANFIS denotes a neuro-fuzzy inference system with adaptive parameter fitting. The study using a black-widow optimizer (BWO) with ANFIS uses association-rule feature selection and separate DUPLEX-based evaluations within two utility datasets; these are within-source experiments rather than a demonstrated transfer between utilities [19]. The uncertainty-oriented fuzzy study instead combines original gas records with perturbed versions before a nominal random partition [20]. Without keeping related variants together, apparent robustness may partly reflect dependence across partitions. Fuzzy learning offers an explicit inference structure, but neither readable rules nor repeated optimization establish independent generalization.
E. Probabilistic Models and Statistical Forecasting
Gaussian-process (GP) classification models gas-to-label relationships through a covariance structure. The supplied Gaussian-process multiclass (GPMC) study uses 7,000 General Electric (GE) utility records across eight categories, with a 4,900/2,100 development/test allocation. Minority discharge categories remain poorly represented despite favorable overall behavior, and labels originate from conventional interpretation [3]. Probabilistic model structure should therefore be separated from evidence that predictive confidence is calibrated under rare faults or changed operating conditions.
The ARIMA framework uses autoregressive integrated moving-average forecasting and addresses a different target: future gas behavior followed by probabilistic interpretation in Duval regions. Its demonstrations use digitized literature trajectories and retrospective forecast origins, rather than a fleet-level supervised classification benchmark. Simultaneous probability assignments and the interpretation of threshold exceedance require additional checks [21]. Forecast intervals, fault-class probabilities, and equipment-failure risk are distinct quantities. These approaches merit evaluation against their respective targets rather than inclusion in a common accuracy ranking.
F. Quantum Classification
The variational quantum classifier (VQC) maps gas measurements through Duval-derived geometry into circuit inputs and evaluates an external IEC set. Its main comparison uses 470 source training cases and 118 external cases across four categories, with training-fitted standardization reported. Hardware assessment uses a smaller subset, and its reported statistic requires denominator verification [22]. The study establishes an experimental pipeline within that scope; differences in hardware subsets, comparator settings, and execution conditions prevent a broad claim of quantum advantage. Representation, simulation, and hardware evidence should remain separate.
G. Model Search and Feature Engineering
Automated machine learning, abbreviated AutoML, and metaheuristics operate across model families. The Auto-WEKA study selects a random forest using development cross-validation, then uses external-benchmark performance for further tuning [23]. An external source consequently can become development data. SSA denotes the sparrow-search algorithm; bat and related optimizers likewise require an explicit account of the data used to choose features, architecture, and parameters [14], [16], [24]. Their names identify search procedures, not independent guarantees of diagnostic improvement.
Across families, features include raw concentrations, ratios, percentages, logarithmic representations, dimensionality reduction, entropy descriptors, and decomposition outputs. HIRA-DGA emphasizes relative gas information [8], whereas Tucker-based selection uses class-informed feature construction [24]. Feature engineering can expose diagnostic structure, but every fitted transformation belongs inside the relevant training partition. Comparing representations is meaningful only when selection and assessment roles are distinguished.
H. Imbalance, Incremental Learning, and Explanation
Imbalance handling changes the training distribution rather than creating new independent fault observations. The GE study compares adaptive synthetic sampling, termed ADASYN, with other oversampling procedures, undersampling, and conditional generation within a fixed classification corpus; augmentation parameters are selected using the test records [7]. The incremental-network study combines oversampling with representation choices, then incorporates IEC records into learning [25]. Those records cannot simultaneously support a claim of untouched external evaluation.
Generative augmentation also requires checking label consistency and separation of originals from their descendants. In the CNN–BiLSTM study, fault labels sampled at random are assigned to generated features; similarity of marginal distributions alone does not establish diagnostic correspondence [17]. The same study uses SHAP, denoting Shapley additive explanations, to explain model predictions. Such attribution supplies a view of fitted behavior, while stability and operational usefulness remain separate evaluation questions. V. Comparative Analysis
A. Dataset Origin, Labels, and Evidence Independence
Table II compares representative pipelines through their data, targets, and evaluation conditions. Its reported values remain study-specific: classification accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, and forecasting outcomes are not interchangeable. The table also distinguishes original measurements from generated rows and separates development records from external assessments. Consequently, its purpose is to explain what each experiment supports, rather than identify a universally strongest method.
Dataset size alone obscures important differences in evidence. The GE classification studies use utility records with conventional-method labels [3], [7], while the GCN experiment combines utility measurements with cases from publications [4]. The AutoML comparison assembles literature and expert-curated cases [12]. These origins imply different opportunities for checking transformer identities, sampling histories, units, and label decisions. A claimed public repository or an on-request availability statement is also insufficient to establish that complete records, preprocessing code, and evaluation partitions can actually be recovered. Table II records access as a source statement, without treating it as an audited release.
Reused corpora further constrain independence. The PCA–ANN and PCA/autoencoder comparison share authors, a described source, class counts, and the same training/test allocation [26], [6]. This is strong evidence of related data provenance, although complete row identity was not independently verified. Similarly, the GE diagnosis and resampling papers use the same diagnostic corpus [3], [7]. Their results can illuminate changes within a related experimental setting; they should not be counted as independent replications. The companion temporal study addresses a separate target within the same publication family [5]. Report count therefore differs from the number of independently observed equipment populations.
B. Class Definitions and Feature Representations
Label provenance determines what successful prediction means. The DNN study learns Duval-generated labels from utility measurements, whereas the ensemble study supplies ratio-derived labels alongside ratio-based predictors [15], [13]. Agreement with those labels primarily measures reproduction of the interpretation scheme. Independently inspected faults would support a different diagnostic claim. Even benchmark names do not guarantee identical targets: the DNN evaluation merges thermal categories, the quantum study uses a reduced class set, and the Auto-WEKA study excludes stray-gassing records [15], [22], [23]. Comparing their aggregate scores without a label crosswalk would conflate different classification problems.
Feature sets likewise differ in what they preserve. Raw concentrations retain magnitude, whereas ratios and normalized compositions emphasize relative gas patterns; engineered descriptors and compressed representations introduce further fitting decisions [3], [8], [6]. HIRA-DGA’s cross-source analysis is especially instructive because undocumented development units complicate the interpretation of magnitude shifts [8]. A transformation that appears robust to one shift cannot establish transfer across incompatible measurement conventions. Representation comparisons should retain units, zero handling, and concentration versus composition distinctions.
The PCA/autoencoder experiment documents training-fitted standardization, but selects compressed dimensions through test behavior [6]. This demonstrates that an isolated scaling step does not make the entire pipeline independent of evaluation data. Feature selection, graph construction, latent-size choice, and architecture search each require their own fitting boundary [4], [24]. Additional descriptors are useful only to the extent that their apparent benefit survives assessment outside the decisions used to construct them.
C. Imbalance and Synthetic Evidence
Overall accuracy can conceal weak recognition of uncommon faults. In the GP study, favorable aggregate performance coexists with poor recognition of the sparsely represented PD category [3]. The AutoML study accordingly reports macro-F1 together with balanced accuracy, while also identifying difficult minority categories [12]. Class-specific recall, class frequencies, and error consequences are needed to interpret an aggregate result. A model evaluated on predominantly normal measurements addresses a different operating distribution from one evaluated on a balanced fault collection.
Resampling changes the evidence used for fitting, rather than the number of independently observed faults. The GE comparison evaluates several balancing procedures but uses the test records to choose augmentation parameters [7]. The GCN paper states that GAN expansion is restricted to training; its subsequent architecture selection nevertheless uses the test results [4]. These are distinct issues and should not be collapsed into a blanket augmentation-leakage claim. For deep models built from hundreds of original cases, augmented row counts should not be treated as equivalent to additional measured faults [4]. Conversely, the fuzzy uncertainty experiment combines originals with simulated variants before random splitting, without reporting grouping by original case [20]. Keeping related rows together is necessary to test robustness on genuinely separate observations.
D. Validation, Metrics, and Transfer
A random record partition does not establish separation between transformers or fault episodes. Stratification preserves class proportions but does not resolve shared operating histories. Cross-validation also depends on whether scaling, selection, and augmentation are fitted within each fold. In the compared pipelines, the relevant distinction is therefore the independence of the complete development process, rather than the presence of a holdout or cross-validation label [6], [12], [24]. Missing grouping information is a limitation of the reported evidence; it does not prove that overlap occurred.
External-source evaluation provides additional information only when its role is preserved. The quantum study reports training-confined fitting and an external IEC assessment, while its hardware subset remains a separate experiment [22]. Auto-WEKA uses benchmark performance for further parameter selection [23], and the incremental network incorporates IEC records into learning [25]. Those target records consequently serve development or adaptation roles. HIRA-DGA retains an untuned external assessment and duplicate-grouped development partitions, but its external score excludes mixed cases and contains no normal examples [8]. It therefore supports a narrower transfer claim than general normal/fault screening.
Temporal evidence requires a separate comparison. The LSTM framework forecasts future gas trajectories from one transformer before classification [5]; the ARIMA framework uses retrospective origins in digitized literature trajectories [21]. Their outcomes cannot be equated with multiclass accuracy from randomly partitioned gas records. Forecast history, horizon, observed fault states, and the distinction between gas error and downstream decisions must remain visible. Table II preserves these contexts and flags unresolved denominators or reporting conflicts. Across the corpus, the useful comparison is between supported diagnostic claims and their evaluation boundaries, not between isolated headline scores.

VI. Critical Discussion
The central limitation of the reviewed evidence is the distance between a reported prediction score and the operational claim it is taken to support. Agreement with gas-rule labels, recognition of inspected faults, transfer to another utility, and prediction of future deterioration require different evidence. The ensemble study’s ratio-derived targets and the DNN study’s Duval-generated labels illustrate the first distinction [13], [15]. Inspection-confirmed IEC cases provide a different reference, but their use for tuning in the Auto-WEKA study limits independent assessment [23]. Standard-related labeling also does not establish compliance with an applicable diagnostic procedure; edition, clause, and implementation checks remain outstanding [STANDARD - VERIFY CLAUSE/YEAR].
Evaluation dependence is a recurring concern with several mechanisms. The failure-detection study explicitly treats multiple measurements from an individual transformer as separate records, while the review could not establish asset-separated evaluation [27]. Synthetic uncertainty variants introduce another dependence structure [20]. Test-assisted selection is documented for GCN settings, augmentation parameters, and latent dimensions [4], [7], [6]. These observations warrant different judgments: documented test use weakens an untouched-test interpretation, whereas unreported grouping leaves independence unresolved. Calling every unclear pipeline “leaky” would exceed the evidence. Conversely, repeated random evaluation cannot by itself repair shared histories or development decisions informed by the test.
Public benchmark evidence remains difficult to reconstruct. Across the 35 empirical reports, the extraction inventory assigned a public-benchmark tag to ten and a private-field tag to 17; these categories overlap and describe provenance claims, not authenticated releases. At least four reports explicitly identify computational artifacts: an AutoML notebook, quantum implementation, fuzzy MATLAB supplement, and HIRA analysis package [12], [22], [20], [8]. This review independently authenticated zero remote data/code releases and executed zero reported experiments. Those procedural limits prevent concluding that named benchmarks or code statements provide complete reproducibility. P12’s on-request data statement and P14’s confidential measurements further distinguish access restrictions from missing methodological details [12], [16].
Reporting consistency is equally consequential. The modified-bat study gives incompatible table and prose summaries [14], while P12’s class frequencies do not reconcile with its stated corpus size [12]. In a simulated-data study, the confusion-matrix total supports the reported overall accuracy, yet class-level metrics and prevalence descriptions remain inconsistent [28]. Such cases should not be reduced to a blanket dismissal or silently corrected into a single preferred number. They require predictions, denominators, and aggregation rules sufficient to identify which result belongs to which experiment. Until resolved, the review can preserve local findings but cannot support a precise cross-paper ordering.
Class imbalance and source shift compound these uncertainties. Rare discharge recognition remains weak in the GP evaluation despite favorable aggregate behavior [3]. HIRA’s duplicate-grouped analysis and untuned external challenge address particular dependence and transfer questions, but its external sample excludes normal cases and separates mixed labels from scored single faults [8]. This supports a limited transfer interpretation. Neither that design nor an external benchmark selected during development establishes general performance across utilities, oil conditions, equipment ages, and sampling practices. Source units, label mappings, and asset histories need to accompany any deployment claim.
Temporal modeling also remains distinct from a prognostic label. The LSTM study evaluates a future gas segment from one transformer [5]; the ARIMA study retrospectively analyzes digitized case histories [21]. A paper framed around prognosis or health assessment can still report classification demonstrations or training error without independently evaluated future failure times, as the Oman study illustrates [29]. Gas-threshold exceedance should therefore not be interpreted as calibrated equipment-failure probability without outcome-based validation. Forecast origin, horizon, fault confirmation, and maintenance interventions determine the meaning of a temporal result.
Interpretability offers a route to inspecting fitted behavior, but trust requires additional evidence. SHAP in the CNN–BiLSTM pipeline explains predictions whose training labels and synthetic-data correspondence also require scrutiny [17]. Fuzzy rules remain subject to contradictory carbon-oxide criteria [18]. Explanations must consequently be assessed alongside label validity, uncertainty, and stability under altered inputs. Likewise, compressed student networks motivate efficient inference, but laptop comparisons do not establish reliable station deployment [16]. Online usefulness requires evidence about changing gas measurements, missing observations, computational resources, and the handling of unsupported fault patterns. The present corpus supports these as evaluation needs; it does not establish that more elaborate models have met them.
A. Coverage Across Representations and Evidence Sources
The wider review literature places these validation concerns within AI/DGA diagnosis [30], mixed diagnostic modalities [31], reliability-oriented interpretation [32], and predictive maintenance [33]. These reviews provide context rather than independent experimental outcomes. Their limitations in data quality, model complexity, measurement reliability, and reconstructable selection procedures reinforce the need for study-level evidence tracing.
Classical and ensemble evidence also includes service-transformer classifiers [34] and stacking/voting pipelines [35]. The former leaves sampling-to-corpus accounting and scaling boundaries unresolved; the latter leaves total corpus size and feature availability unclear. South African [36] and Tunisian [37] SVM cascades further show that hierarchical decisions require stage-specific denominators and declared selection boundaries before transfer claims can be assessed.
Representation-based pipelines extend beyond PCA and autoencoders. Entropy features with an optimized CNN [38] and rough-set reduction before a neural network [39] introduce additional fitting decisions. Confidential measurements and unestablished nested selection constrain the former; conflicting test totals and fault-code descriptions constrain the latter. Neither feature reduction nor cross-validation alone resolves these reporting and independence questions.
Classifier fusion introduces another selection layer. Hypersphere classification combined with Dempster–Shafer evidence [40] uses engineered gas representations and optimized constituent models, but selected settings and class-level reports conflict. A transparent fusion operator cannot compensate for an assessment partition used to choose its inputs or thresholds; those decisions require separate development evidence.
Empirical-mode decomposition [41] and intrinsic time-scale decomposition [42] supply alternative descriptors to XGBoost. Their shared authors, source datasets, and near-matching counts make the comparison a related-corpus investigation. Split descriptions and table denominators remain inconsistent, so the studies broaden representation coverage without establishing independent replication or supporting a cross-paper accuracy ordering.
Longitudinal gas vectors in ANN, SVM, and nearest-neighbor models [43] differ from prospective forecasting: combining annual observations does not establish an independently evaluated future horizon. Likewise, ANN emulation of Rogers and IEC codes [44] addresses interpretation-scheme agreement, while conflicting summaries and illustrative-case evaluation limit inspected-fault claims. Target definition therefore remains consequential across both temporal inputs and rule-derived labels.
The supplied doctoral thesis [45] provides additional provenance: its publication list explicitly links the GE classification, resampling, and forecasting papers, while its database chapter describes repeated asset monitoring and conventional-rule labeling. It is cited as supplementary methodological evidence, not another empirical replication. The bibliography consequently covers all 44 eligible core reports and one supplementary thesis while preserving the distinction between source count and independent evidence.
VII. Future Research Directions
Table III connects the observed limitations to proposed research designs and explicit evaluation criteria.
A. Traceable Labels and Reproducible Benchmarks
Rule-derived targets and conflicting dataset accounts complicate diagnostic interpretation [13], [12]. A benchmark should distinguish inspected faults from algorithmic labels and provide asset identifiers, measurement units, class mappings, and immutable data partitions. Publish transformation code and predictions alongside permitted records or a controlled-access evaluation interface. Assess reproducibility through independent reruns and agreement on reconstructed counts, labels, and metrics.
B. Evaluation Independent of Model Selection
Test-assisted tuning and separately treated measurements from shared assets weaken independence [4], [7], [27]. Use transformer- or fault-episode groups for development and final assessment, with preprocessing and parameter selection fitted inside nested development folds. Reserve a final evaluation partition before model search. Compare record-level and grouped results, report variation across development runs, and identify every decision that used assessment data.
C. Training-Confined Augmentation and Minority-Fault Evaluation
Rare discharge weakness and uncertain synthetic-label correspondence require targeted checks [3], [17]. Fit generators and resampling procedures only within development folds, preserving source/descendant groups and conditioning generated measurements on verified categories. Compare augmentation with class weighting and unaugmented baselines on real, grouped holdouts. Report minority recall, false alarms, and confusion patterns; test whether gains survive removal of synthetic observations from assessment.
D. Cross-Source Transfer and Calibrated Abstention
Benchmark tuning, target adaptation, and external sets without normal cases support different transfer claims [23], [25], [8]. Establish cross-utility evaluations with harmonized units and independently confirmed labels, declaring target-use policies before fitting. Separate adaptation data from final target assessment. Evaluate class-specific performance, probability calibration, and the balance between accepted decisions and their errors, including normal, mixed, and unsupported fault cases.
E. Prospective Temporal Assessment
Single-transformer forecasts and retrospective digitized trajectories provide limited temporal evidence [5], [21]. Evaluate gas forecasts and downstream diagnosis at successive future origins across independent assets, preserving sampling intervals and documenting maintenance interventions. Compare with simple persistence and statistical baselines, separating concentration error from fault recognition. Evaluate interval coverage by horizon; reserve failure-risk or remaining-life claims for studies linked to independently observed outcomes.
F. Explanations and Online Decision Support
SHAP attribution and network compression have not established station-level reliability [17], [16]. Test explanation stability under correlated gas inputs and realistic measurement perturbations, then assess whether operators can identify questionable predictions. Evaluate inference on intended devices using latency, memory, missing-data handling, and abstention behavior. A monitored pilot should record overrides and confirmed fault outcomes, connecting computational efficiency and explanations to auditable decisions.

VIII. Conclusion
This corpus-based review examined 44 eligible DGA reports, comprising 35 empirical studies and nine reviews, through a taxonomy that separates diagnostic targets, learning methods, data handling, and evaluation independence. Across the represented classifier families, comparisons remain meaningful only within compatible data and validation settings; reported scores do not establish a universally preferable learner. Dataset reuse, rule-derived labels, differences in class definitions, fitted feature transformations, and synthetic augmentation influence what results demonstrate, while aggregate accuracy can obscure minority-fault errors. Reproducibility and transfer remain limited by unverified releases, inconsistent reporting, uncertain asset separation, and benchmark use during development; external and temporal demonstrations support narrower claims than fleet-wide diagnosis or failure prognosis. The resulting research priorities are traceable labels and executable benchmarks, grouped independent assessment, training-confined augmentation, explicit cross-source adaptation policies, prospective forecasting, and evaluation of explanations and inference on intended devices. These conclusions apply to the supplied corpus and its documented limitations, with original search provenance still unavailable and no independent reproduction of the reported experiments.
